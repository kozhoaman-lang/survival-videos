You open your eyes... and the air is hot, wet, and loud. Next to your hand there's a footprint. It's bigger than you are. You're in North America. Sixty-six million years ago. The age of Tyrannosaurus rex. You have to survive seven days. And here's the part nobody tells you... The T. rex is not the scariest thing you'll meet this week. Not even close.

First, where are you? Scientists call this place Hell Creek. Today it's dry badlands in Montana and the Dakotas. But right now it's a warm, swampy lowland... full of rivers, ferns, conifers, and some of the very first flowering plants. Look around. Something is missing. There are no open meadows of grass. The grassy plains you know haven't spread across the world yet. And it's hot. Even in winter, this place barely gets cold. That sounds like good news. It isn't. Heat means you lose water fast. And water is where your first problem starts.

Day one. You need water... and you can hear a river. You walk toward it. Brown, slow, calm. Too calm. Hell Creek's rivers were home to crocodile relatives and alligator-like hunters. They waited right where thirsty animals came to drink. Animals like you. So you don't drink from the edge. Instead, you go a few steps back from the bank, where the ground is sandy, and you dig. A shallow hole. Slowly, water seeps in from the river... filtered through the sand. It's still not perfectly safe. But it's cleaner, and you didn't have to put your face next to something with teeth. You drink. You survived day one. But tonight, you'll learn this world doesn't sleep.

Day two. You're starving. Your first idea: eat plants. Bad idea. You don't know a single one of them. Some ferns are toxic raw, even today. In a world you've never seen, a mistake with plants can kill you slower... but just as surely. Your second idea: dinosaur eggs. You find a nest. The eggs are huge. Stop. Ask yourself one question... where is the mother? Many dinosaurs guarded their nests. Stealing eggs is the fastest way to meet an adult. So you do what early humans did best. You go small. Turtles basking on logs. Fish in the shallows. Freshwater clams. Hell Creek was full of them. The safest meal in the age of dinosaurs... isn't a dinosaur at all.

Day three. You hear chewing. Through the ferns you see it. Triceratops. One of the most common big dinosaurs in Hell Creek. It eats plants. It looks calm. You think: this one's safe. That thought gets people killed... even today. In Africa, the animals that kill the most people aren't lions. Hippos and buffalo are among the most dangerous animals on the continent. Plant-eaters. Big, nervous, and protective. Now imagine one nine meters long, weighing several tons... with three horns and a bony shield. It doesn't need to hunt you. It only needs to feel threatened. You back away slowly. You never turn your back. You never get between a herd and its young. And when you're far enough away... you realize something else is moving in the trees. Something that's been following you.

Day four. You're being watched. Forget the giant raptors from the movies. The real hunters of Hell Creek were smaller. One of them is called Acheroraptor. Feathered. Fast. About the size of a big dog... with a curved claw on each foot. A big dinosaur might ignore you. To something this size, you're exactly the right size. Your choice: run or climb. You climb. Raptors were built for running, not climbing tall trees. You wedge yourself high between two branches and you stay there... all night. Below you: clicks. Chirps. Footsteps that stop... and start again. At sunrise, they're gone. You come down shaking. You think the worst is over. But something already got you. You just can't feel it yet.

Day five. You wake up with a fever. Remember the footprint? Remember the raptors? None of them got you. Something tiny did. Ticks were already feeding on feathered dinosaurs tens of millions of years before this day. Scientists have found them trapped in amber, holding on to a dinosaur feather. Mosquitoes were here too. And then there are the microbes. Bacteria and parasites that evolved in a world with no humans. Your body has never met any of them. Nobody knows exactly what they would do to you. And that's the scary part. You spend the day in the shade, drinking from your seep hole, keeping your cuts clean with boiled... no. You have no pot. You have no fire. You just wait... and hope. By night, the fever breaks. And for the first time since you woke up, the jungle goes completely quiet.

Day six. Silence means one thing in this world. Something big is coming. Tyrannosaurus rex. Twelve meters long. One of the strongest bites of any land animal that ever lived. And you remember the movies: stand still... it can't see you if you don't move. That's a myth. Studies of its skull suggest T. rex had forward-facing eyes with good depth perception... and possibly sharp eyesight. It also had huge smell centers in its brain. It doesn't need you to move. It can smell you. So standing still is the worst thing you can do. Here's the one thing on your side: size. A full-grown T. rex was so heavy that some scientists estimate its top speed at only around twelve miles per hour. A fit human can sprint faster than that... for a short time. But young tyrannosaurs were lighter, leaner... and probably much faster. So you don't run in the open. You run where it can't follow. Through thick trees. Across the river. Anything that breaks its line of sight and its path. You dive into the water... and you stay under as long as you can. When you come up... it's gone. You did it. Six days. One more and you've survived the age of dinosaurs. Tomorrow should be easy.

Day seven. Spring morning. The air is soft. The animals are calm. And then... far to the south, the sky does something wrong. Remember how this started? Sixty-six million years ago. That's not just a date. That's THE date. Three thousand kilometers away, on what will one day be Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, a rock about ten kilometers wide has just hit the Earth. You can't see the impact from here. But within minutes, the ground starts to shake. Then something falls from the sky. Not rain. Tiny beads of glass... melted rock, thrown into space and raining back down. This isn't imagination. In North Dakota, at a fossil site called Tanis, scientists found fish with those glass beads stuck in their gills. They were breathing them in... as they died. The river beside you rises and rushes backward over its banks. Some scientists believe the sky itself may have grown hot as debris fell back through the atmosphere. The dinosaurs around you have ruled this planet for more than one hundred and fifty million years. They have only hours left.

You made it seven days. You beat the crocodiles. The raptors. The fever. The king of the dinosaurs. But the dinosaurs couldn't survive what's coming next. And you're still here.
