Saturday afternoon. An easy hike in the woods. You step off the trail for one minute — just to take a photo. When you turn around, the trail is gone. No signal. Two hours until sunset. Seven days alone in the forest. Here's how you survive every one of them.

Your first instinct is to run and find the way out. Don't. Rescuers teach a simple rule: STOP. S — stay calm. Breathe, take a sip of water. T — think. How did you get here? O — observe. Look for footprints and landmarks. P — plan. Only head back if you're sure of the way.

If you don't know where you are, stay where you are. Searchers start at the place you were last seen. A person who stays put gets found faster than one who wanders — and doesn't fall off a cliff. That's why it matters to tell someone your plan before you go. Rescuers even teach kids: if you're lost, hug a tree.

The thing most likely to kill you out here isn't a bear. It's the cold. Hypothermia can hit even above forty degrees, if you're wet from rain or sweat. First comes shivering. Then clumsiness and confusion. And when the shivering stops — that's a very bad sign.

While there's still light, build a shelter. Pick a spot out of the wind, close to a small clearing. Got a trash bag? Cut a hole for your face and wear it like a poncho. The face hole is a must — without it, you can suffocate. And never lie on bare ground. It pulls the heat right out of you. Pile up a thick bed of dry leaves and branches.

Day two. You can last weeks without food — but only a few days without water. A stream nearby is good luck, but even clear water can be full of germs. If you have something to boil it in, keep it at a rolling boil for at least one minute. Above six thousand five hundred feet, make it three. And don't wander far from camp.

Fire gives you warmth, safe water and a signal — all at once. In the daytime, add green leaves or grass to make thick smoke that can be seen from far away. At night, keep the flames bright. Ring the fire with rocks, and never leave it alone. A wildfire is the last thing you need.

Day three. A helicopter in the distance… and it flies right past. From the air, a person under the trees is almost invisible. Remember the rule of three: three of anything is a universal distress signal. Three whistle blasts carry farther than your voice, and take less energy. No whistle? Three shouts work too. A mirror flashing sunlight can be seen for miles. Spell out SOS with rocks and branches — letters about twelve feet tall. And spread bright clothing out in the open.

Your phone is still your most valuable tool, even with no signal. Keep it in airplane mode, and keep it warm, close to your body — cold drains the battery faster. Once a day, climb to the nearest open high spot, without losing sight of camp, and check for service. One bar is enough to call 911.

By day four, the hunger is brutal. And this is where people make a deadly mistake. Never eat wild mushrooms or berries unless you are one hundred percent sure. One bite can be enough. Meanwhile, a person can survive three to four weeks without food. Hunger feels terrible. But it's not what kills you.

Night five. A branch snaps in the dark. Your mind imagines the worst. Blow your whistle. It will scare off an animal — and if a searcher is nearby, they'll hear you. By now, your most dangerous enemy is despair. Tell yourself: people are already looking for me. Give yourself a routine: water, firewood, signals. Simple tasks keep your mind busy — and keep you alive.

Day six. Voices in the distance. It sounds strange, but lost people sometimes hide from rescuers — out of fear, or embarrassment. Nobody is going to be angry with you. Most searchers are volunteers, and they're looking for you. Step into the open and blow your whistle three times.

Day seven. Another helicopter. This time, you're ready. Run to the clearing. Green branches on the fire. Wave both arms over your head. Smoke, SOS on the ground, a bright jacket — and the helicopter turns around. You've been found. Because you stayed put, and helped them find you.

Seven days in the forest. Here's what saved you. You stopped, and didn't panic. You stayed put. You stayed warm and dry. You boiled your water. You signaled in threes. And you didn't eat a single mystery mushroom.

One last thing. Before any hike, tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back. And pack a whistle, a small mirror and a trash bag. They weigh almost nothing — and they can save your life. Subscribe, and I'll see you in the next survival scenario.
